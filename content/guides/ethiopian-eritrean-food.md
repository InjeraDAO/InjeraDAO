# Ethiopian and Eritrean food: a beginner's guide

When you sit down for Ethiopian or Eritrean food, you may see a large injera-lined platter covered with colorful vegetable dishes and stews. The variety is the invitation: pick something familiar, try something new and share a conversation.

## The foundation: injera
Injera is a fermented flatbread with a soft texture and characteristic holes. It can act as plate, accompaniment and utensil.

## Common dishes to explore
- **Shiro:** A seasoned dish commonly made from ground legumes, especially chickpeas or broad beans. Recipes vary.
- **Misir wot / misir wat:** A spiced lentil preparation; ingredient lists and heat levels differ by cook.
- **Gomen:** Cooked greens, often collards or similar leaves, with regional variations.
- **Atakilt:** Vegetable combinations often featuring cabbage, potato and carrot; names and preparations vary.
- **Doro wot / doro wat:** A richly seasoned chicken stew often served for special occasions, with variations.
- **Tibs:** Sautéed meat dishes found in many styles; not all versions are identical.
- **Ful:** Fava bean dishes enjoyed in different forms across the region.
- **Zigni:** A seasoned stew closely associated with Eritrean food traditions, with many household approaches.

Spelling varies with transliteration and language. Menu descriptions should always reflect the individual kitchen.

## Vegetarian and fasting foods
A wide range of vegetable and legume dishes make Ethiopian and Eritrean menus especially interesting to explore. However, do not assume every dish is vegan: check whether it contains butter, stock or other animal-derived ingredients.

## Trying a restaurant for the first time
Ask about spice level, whether dishes are served as individual portions or on a shared platter, and what kind of injera is available. If you have allergies, dietary restrictions or gluten sensitivity, ask staff about ingredients and cross-contact.
